Bargaining theory, quickly

A strategic map of who gets what

A bargain starts with a surplus that can be created through agreement and a disagreement outcome if talks fail. Theory asks how the protocol, patience, information, alternatives, and social norms shape both the division and whether agreement happens at all.

01

The surplus

The value created by agreement. A larger pie does not determine how it will be divided.

02

Outside options

What each side receives after no deal. A credible walk-away option changes bargaining power.

03

The protocol

Who offers first, who may counter, and when time runs out can redirect the final split.

04

Information & norms

Private values, fairness, focal points, and communication explain why real deals depart from narrow predictions.

The Bargaining ShaftAn interactive teaching project grounded in empirical bargaining research.

Rules of the shaft

Mine hard. Bargain harder.

  1. Choose a mechanism. Use alternating offers, an ultimatum, simultaneous claims, a costly deadline, or a meet-halfway rule. The first mover changes each round.
  2. Mine together. Drop each swinging hook to collect gold, gems, and rocks before time expires.
  3. With a deal: each player keeps a 30% finder’s bonus from their own haul. The remaining 70% is pooled, receives a cooperation bonus, and follows the negotiated split.
  4. Without a deal: no pooling. Each player keeps their own haul after a 15% conflict tax.
  5. Win the match. The richest miner after four rounds wins.

Bargaining laboratory

Five rules. Five incentive systems.

Alternating offers

Offer, reject, counter. First-mover advantage survives in experiments, while exact equilibrium splits often do not.

Take-it-or-leave-it

One proposal, then accept or reject. Fairness, stakes, and information all affect ultimatum behavior.

Simultaneous claims

Compatible sealed demands settle; over-claiming destroys the contract.

Deadline bargaining

Counteroffers are allowed, but the cooperation bonus falls while players wait.

Meet halfway

Both players anchor a position; the rule chooses their midpoint, a common field norm.

Message buttons are payoff-neutral “cheap talk.” Field evidence from eBay found that enabling messages reduced bargaining breakdown, especially after users learned what to say.
